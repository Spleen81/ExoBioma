The project · The framework · The first paper · The database

Life does not inhabit
a planet. It rewrites it.

Exobioma is an astrobiology project that brings together the environmental tolerances of 300 extremophiles and atmospheric models of exoplanets in the NASA Exoplanet Archive. Its MLS metric assesses how compatible each planetary environment may be with extremophile life. The long-term goal is to link microbial metabolism to atmospheric chemistry, identifying molecules that future observatories such as JWST, ARIEL and HWO could search for.

§ 01

The project

Exobioma starts from what we know about life on Earth and extends that knowledge to the search for life beyond our planet — in atmospheres, on surfaces and beneath them. Its ultimate aim is observational verification. An exobioma is the microbial community that a given extraterrestrial environment could potentially support, shaped by its physical and chemical conditions.

A

The metric

Life-Compatible Zone and Multiparametric Life Score: no longer “is the planet habitable?”, but a continuous ranking that assesses and describes the compatibility of an exoplanetary atmosphere with life.

B

The microbiology

The database, already at version 2.0, contains 300 extremophiles with tolerances extended to chemistry, metabolic compounds and a metagenomic complement. It remains a work in progress, with the aim of adding new bacterial species and new tolerance parameters.

C

The models

The next step is to couple microbial metabolism with atmospheric chemistry, translating the MLS compatibility score into a planetary biochemical reaction network. This is also the starting point for the dynamic branch, MLS(φ), which follows the evolution of the Multiparametric Life Score along a planet's orbit. The goal is to identify temporal variations in volatile organic compounds that future observatories such as HWO may be able to detect over multiple orbital epochs.

WHY NOW With JWST now operating and ARIEL and HWO on the horizon, the characterization of rocky-planet atmospheres is becoming one of the most promising avenues in the search for life. Exobioma aims to build a quantitative bridge between microbiological knowledge and exoplanet observations, helping guide the selection of future targets.
HOW WE POSITION OURSELVES Exobioma enters an already active line of work. Earth Similarity Indices (ESI) and their adaptation to extremophiles (Schulze-Makuch et al., 2011; Jagadeesh, 2018) use few planetary parameters with a terrestrial reference. The analysis of the topology of atmospheric reaction networks as a possible biosignature was proposed by Solé and Munteanu (2004) and developed by Wong et al. (2023) and Fisher et al. (2025). Models that couple microbial communities and the atmosphere exist for the Archean and for individual planets (Kharecha et al., 2005; Sauterey et al., 2020; Bishop et al., 2024), and the lack of ecosystem context in biomass models is acknowledged by Seager et al. (2013). Our contribution is to build on a broader database of 300 species, each characterized by multiparametric environmental tolerances: this allows us to identify the microbial communities compatible with different planetary environments and to extend the analysis across the updated exoplanet catalog.
§ 02

The two databases behind the matching

MLS is not a property of the planet: it is the result of a comparison. Computing it requires two independent outputs. The first describes the environment — vertical profiles of temperature, pressure, composition and UV/X flux, generated for every planet in the NASA Exoplanet Archive. The second describes the biological constraints — the environmental tolerances of terrestrial extremophiles. Without the first there is nowhere to search; without the second there is nothing to compare against. The engine generates the profiles, the MLS 2.0 database provides the biological tolerances, and the MLS calculator is the point where the two inputs become a score.

Input 2 · Biosphere

Tolerance as a biological yardstick

The second input comes from biology. The MLS 2.0 database gathers 300 extremophile species with multiparametric tolerances, compounds associated with metabolism and genomic data. It is the part of the framework that turns a physical profile into a biological question: which species could remain active in that layer, and over which part of the column.

300

Reference extremophiles, photosynthetic and chemolithotrophic, with their tolerance profiles: 60 species for each physical category (thermophiles, psychrophiles, barophiles, radioresistant) and 15 for each chemical category.

Temperature, pressure, radiation (UV and X), pH, salinity, desiccation and water activity, ion chemistry (sulfates, perchlorates): 20 quantities per species, plus the maximum growth rate.

Status: version 2.0 consolidated, expanding. It feeds the MLS metric, the second paper on Mars and Enceladus and — as the next step — the coupling code with atmospheric chemistry. The first paper uses version 1 of the database (200 species).

THE URGENCY OF NEW EXPERIMENTAL DATA FROM LABORATORY TESTS About 70% of the records are not a direct experimental measurement: they are estimates derived from origin habitats or inferences from the literature. Moreover the database reflects the cultivable and best-studied species, which are a small part of microbial diversity. For this reason we plan a validation program: low-cost proliferation tests under UV, X-rays, temperature and pressure, and genomic profiling to consolidate metabolic pathways. Every measurement that extends the known limits of survival widens the set of compatible planets.
The matching

Where the two databases meet

mls.py reads the profiles in the engine's export format — ID, T_C, P_atm, UV, X, Vol, Z_km, Δh — and compares them, layer by layer, with the tolerances of the 300 species. For each species–layer pair it computes three coefficients (temperature, pressure, radiation), combines them into by a geometric mean, integrates the column and normalizes over active species and thickness; rewards metabolic diversity. The result is one MLS per planet–profile pair: 7,653 profiles × 300 species ≈ 2.3 million pairs, each over 50 layers. A high score is not proof of life: it is an ordering of plausibility under stated assumptions.

§ 03

The paradigm shift

For a long time habitability and the biosphere were treated in sequence: first one establishes whether a planet can host life, then one discusses what life would produce. We hope for the passage from a static view — the planet permits life — to a dynamic one — life shapes the planet. Coupled ecosystem–atmosphere models exist for the Archean and for a handful of planets. Exobioma proposes to extend this approach to a broad community of extremophiles, selected on the basis of orbital parameters and of the atmospheric data that the next instruments will provide.

Static view

The planet as a precondition: an index of compatibility orders worlds.

Dynamic view

The biosphere as part of the system: life changes the composition of the atmosphere.

01

LCS & MLS — the metric

The Life-Compatible Shell measures how much of the atmosphere has pressures compatible with known extremophiles; the Multiparametric Life Score merges LCS and profiles (T, P, radiation) into a score that orders planets. MLS does not replace the habitable zone: it widens its criterion, describing planets through the tolerances of extremophiles in the 3 physical dimensions considered.

02

Fluid-dynamic taxonomy

Working hypothesis: at high pressure the density of an atmosphere can approach that of living matter, reducing sedimentation. In this regime cells could remain suspended at a reduced energetic cost (neutral buoyancy regime). The thresholds depend on composition and temperature and are illustrated in §05.

03

Thermodynamic black box

Microbial metabolism is treated as a source term in the continuity equations of chemical reaction networks (CRN). The formalism is defined (§05); its implementation in atmospheric-chemistry codes is in development.

THE LEGACY OF DAISYWORLD — WATSON AND LOVELOCK, 1983 Planetary regulation emerges spontaneously from biological feedback: even a mat of dark cyanobacteria alters the albedo and the thermal balance of a world. And the Habitable Zone, on its own, is not enough: the subglacial oceans of Europa and Enceladus host liquid water far from the star thanks to tidal heating, and a strong greenhouse effect can move the energy balance of a planet well beyond the canonical distances. Exobioma draws on the logic of modern Earth System Models: life not as a taxonomic list, but as a flux-exchange module coupled to the atmospheric code. Daisyworld acts on albedo; here the coupling concerns the gases exchanged with the atmosphere.
Inside the metric

How MLS arises: the formula, layer by layer

MLS is not a judgment assigned a priori: it is an integral. For every species and every altitude , the local compatibility compares temperature, pressure and radiation with the known tolerances. The integral measures how much column thickness that species can inhabit; rewards columns in which several metabolic categories coexist, while weights the volume of the shell. The result, raised to , stays between 0 and 1 and becomes a number comparable between planets.

§ 04

Why extremophiles

The life limits of extremophiles are already used to assess where life might exist elsewhere (for example McKay, 2014; Hallsworth et al., 2021). Exobioma gathers them in a structured database and compares them systematically with known planets.

01

They redefine the habitable

Known activity and growth temperatures range from about −20 °C (Psychromonas, activity) to over 120 °C (Methanopyrus kandleri, 122 °C): almost 140 degrees. To these are added intense radiation and pressures up to ≈1400 bar. These limits have widened with each discovery, but they remain limits: water activity below ≈0.585, for instance, has not so far been observed to be tolerated (Hallsworth et al., 2021).

02

Planetary engineers

Microorganisms, extremophiles included, take part in biogeochemical cycles and, as endolithic bacteria do, break down rock creating micro-habitats. On a planetary scale they can therefore modify the environment in which they live.

03

They live where we can look

The Earth's atmosphere hosts active microorganisms: growth and degradation of organic compounds such as formaldehyde have been observed in cloud and fog droplets (Amato et al., 2007; Cao et al., 2026). Aerial life cycles have been proposed for the clouds of Venus, but the very low water activity of sulfuric-acid droplets makes them problematic (Hallsworth et al., 2021). Experiments with E. coli show that the composition of the surrounding gas (H₂, CO₂, CH₄) influences growth [VERIFY].

From the exobiome to the CRN

The thermodynamic black box

For the source term the cell is treated as a thermodynamic black box: what matters are the input fluxes and the outgoing gases, in the continuity equations of the atmospheric CRNs. The chain is explicit: comes from the LCS shell (§05, where), the flux comes from the prototypes (§05, who · what), and their product is the term that life adds to the code. The detail of the pathways, derived from the genomic complement, is needed to determine the stoichiometric yields. Move the pointer over the symbols to read their role.

The dynamic branch

The blind zone of the orbit: what the coronagraph cannot see

So far the metric has described an instantaneous atmosphere: one MLS per planet, with no need to cover the orbit. In the dynamic branch (§06) the question changes: no longer a single number, but a curve MLS(φ) along the whole orbit. It is only in this technique that phase coverage becomes part of the problem: the limit is no longer only biological or chemical, but also optical. To follow the signal phase by phase one has to see the planet at those phases.

Direct imaging separates the light of the planet from that of the star with a coronagraph. Below a certain angular separation — the inner working angle (inner working angle, IWA) — the planet remains buried in the stellar residual. In an eccentric orbit the projected separation is minimal near periapsis, where stellar irradiation and orbital speed reach their maximum: the scheduled epochs that fall in that arc form a blind zone adjacent to periapsis.

The constraint applies to every direct-imaging telescope with a coronagraph — HWO, the Coronagraph Instrument of Roman, the high-contrast instruments of the ELT — and, in a different form, to any technique that must angularly separate planet and star. For Exobioma it is a sampling constraint: the MLS(φ) curve is defined over all phases, but observational verification covers only the accessible arc. If precisely the periapsis phase is missing, the model–observation comparison must be read in the light of the phases actually sampled.

OPERATIONAL CONSEQUENCE — CHOOSING THE TARGET The blind zone does not say “there is no life here”: it says “here, with this instrument, we cannot verify it”. This is a substantial distinction from the abiotic case, in which it is the compatible column that is lacking and the model has nothing to compare. For this reason the choice of target matters as much as the model: for a given signal it is preferable to choose low-eccentricity orbits, wide semimajor axes or favourable inclinations (orbits seen nearly pole-on), which keep the planet outside the IWA over a larger fraction of the orbit.
§ 06

From microbe to observation

From the microbe to the model, from the model to the molecule, from the molecule to the observation: the pathway that links microbiological data, atmospheric models and observations, with the results returning into the database.

01 · MICROBE

The database

300 extremophiles with tolerance parameters, associated compounds and genomic complement: the foundation from which the biochemical networks will be built, taking the metabolic network of E. coli — the best annotated one — as a reference.

02 · MODEL

The atmospheric CRN

Gas fluxes from compatible microbial metabolisms are introduced into the atmospheric reaction network. It is the code under development, the next step of the framework.

03 · MOLECULE

The VOC list

The algorithm identifies the volatile species that differ most from the corresponding abiotic atmosphere: candidates as traces to search for, planet by planet.

04 · OBSERVATION

The HWO validation

The lists guide the choice of targets and of the spectral bands to observe with JWST, ARIEL and HWO.HWO

GENOMIC BRANCH FIRST TESTS PRELIMINARY

From species to function: metagenomics

The database associates each species with its genomic complement. First preliminary tests indicate that it is possible to describe how genes — and hence metabolic functions — are distributed across the niches of an atmosphere, moving from a description by species to one by function. This is a prediction based on the tolerances of the carrier species, not an observation: it will be verified by comparing it with environmental metagenomic data from clouds and fogs. The aim is to understand how much the results depend on the number and composition of the species represented.

  • Gene stratification of atmospheric niches: preliminary tests
  • Validation on environmental metagenomes (clouds, fogs)
  • From pathways per species to functional modules per niche
